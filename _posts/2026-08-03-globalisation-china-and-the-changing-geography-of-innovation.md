# Globalisation, China and the Changing Geography of Innovation

For much of the past forty years, globalisation was described through a simple division of labour. Western economies generated ideas, brands and intellectual property, while China supplied factories, workers and industrial scale. Few products represented this arrangement more clearly than the iPhone. The phrase “Designed by Apple in California” suggested that the creativity and strategic value originated in the United States, while final assembly took place primarily in China.

That description was never entirely complete. Apple products depend on a global network of component manufacturers, engineers, software developers, logistics providers and assembly partners. Apple itself now states that its products are designed in California and built by people around the world. Nevertheless, China became central to the system because it could combine skilled labour, sophisticated suppliers, infrastructure and enormous production capacity in a way that few other countries could match.

The significance of the iPhone is therefore not that China copied an American product. China helped make the American product commercially possible. Apple provided the brand, operating system, product vision and customer experience, but Chinese manufacturing networks supplied the scale, speed and production knowledge required to deliver hundreds of millions of devices. The iPhone was not purely American or Chinese. It was a product of globalisation.

Artificial intelligence may be following a similar path, but with one crucial difference. China is no longer limited to assembly. Chinese companies are increasingly designing, training and releasing their own large language models. The country is moving from being a manufacturing centre within Western led systems to becoming an important creator of technological systems in its own right.

According to information presented at the 2025 World Artificial Intelligence Conference, China had released 1,509 large artificial intelligence models, representing a substantial share of the global total reported at the event. Chinese regulators also reported that more than 700 generative AI model products had completed official filing procedures by the end of 2025. These figures should be interpreted carefully because not every registered model is a frontier model. Even so, they demonstrate the scale and intensity of AI development taking place across Chinese universities, technology companies and research laboratories.

The Stanford AI Index provides a more selective comparison. It found that American institutions produced 40 notable AI models in 2024, compared with 15 from China. However, it also found that the performance difference between leading American and Chinese models had narrowed sharply, reaching near parity on several important benchmarks. China also continued to lead in the overall volume of AI publications and patents.

This development is often described as China copying the West. That interpretation is too simplistic. It is true that many important concepts behind modern artificial intelligence were developed or commercialised by American institutions. The transformer architecture, large scale language modelling, consumer chatbots and commercial model platforms were all advanced significantly by Western researchers and companies. Chinese developers have studied these breakthroughs, adopted successful methods and built competing systems.

However, learning from an existing technology is not the same as merely copying it. Almost every industrial power has developed through observation, adaptation and improvement. American manufacturing learned from European industry. Japanese car companies studied American mass production before developing their own production systems. South Korean electronics companies entered markets previously dominated by American and Japanese firms. Innovation rarely happens in isolation. It usually develops through the circulation of ideas, people, capital and technical knowledge.

China has repeatedly demonstrated a particular ability to absorb a proven concept, construct the infrastructure needed to produce it at scale, reduce its cost and adapt it to different markets. This ability is sometimes dismissed as imitation, but industrial execution is itself a form of innovation. Building a successful product requires more than possessing the original idea. It requires supply chains, engineering discipline, manufacturing knowledge, distribution, customer support and continuous improvement.

The competition between Tesla and BYD illustrates this process. Tesla played a central role in transforming the electric car from a niche environmental product into a desirable technological product. It demonstrated that electric vehicles could offer performance, software integration and strong consumer appeal. BYD followed a different route. It developed deep expertise in batteries and manufacturing, produced vehicles across a broad range of prices and brought much of its supply chain under direct control.

By 2025, BYD reported sales of more than 2.25 million battery electric passenger vehicles, while Tesla reported approximately 1.64 million vehicle deliveries. The comparison is not perfect because the companies have different product ranges, reporting methods and business structures. Nevertheless, the broader direction is clear. BYD is no longer simply a Chinese alternative attempting to reproduce Tesla. It has become a major global competitor with its own battery technology, production strategy, product portfolio and international ambitions.

The same pattern may now be appearing in artificial intelligence. Western companies established the first widely recognised generation of consumer large language models. Chinese companies are now competing through efficiency, accessibility, local language performance, open model releases and integration with domestic platforms. Restrictions on access to the most advanced computer chips may even encourage Chinese developers to focus more intensely on efficient training, software optimisation and specialised hardware.

This does not mean that concerns about intellectual property should be ignored. There is an important distinction between learning from a public research paper, building upon open source software and extracting protected knowledge from a commercial system without permission. Questions surrounding training data, model distillation, patents and trade secrets require clear international rules. Admiring China’s capacity for industrial development should not require accepting every method used by every company.

At the same time, describing all Chinese technological progress as copying prevents Western countries from understanding the real competitive challenge. The important question is not whether China began with ideas developed elsewhere. The important question is what Chinese companies are able to do with those ideas. A competitor that can reproduce an existing capability, lower its cost, improve its efficiency and distribute it across a vast market may eventually become more influential than the original inventor.

The changing role of China also exposes a contradiction within globalisation. Western companies benefited enormously from access to Chinese manufacturing, suppliers, workers and consumers. That relationship helped reduce production costs and increase corporate profits. Yet the same process also transferred knowledge, developed local expertise and created powerful industrial ecosystems. China did not remain a passive workshop. Its engineers, managers and entrepreneurs learned how global products were designed, produced and commercialised.

The iPhone therefore represents the first stage of this story. It was designed in California and assembled largely through Asian supply chains, with China occupying a central position. The next stage is represented by companies such as BYD and by China’s growing AI sector. Products may increasingly be designed, engineered and manufactured in China before being sold throughout the world.

This is not the end of Western innovation, nor does it guarantee Chinese technological dominance. The United States still possesses exceptional universities, capital markets, semiconductor capabilities, software companies and research institutions. China faces its own challenges, including access to advanced chips, regulatory controls, demographic pressures and international distrust. The future is more likely to involve continuous competition and interdependence than complete domination by either side.

Globalisation has never been simply about moving factories to cheaper locations. It is also a mechanism through which knowledge spreads and capabilities develop. The West helped create the modern global technology system, while China became exceptionally skilled at operating within it. China is now using the expertise gained from that system to build its own products, companies and technological platforms.

The lesson from the iPhone, BYD and large language models is therefore not that China merely copies what the West creates. It is that technological leadership is rarely permanent. An idea may originate in one country, be manufactured in another, improved somewhere else and eventually transformed by a new competitor. In a globalised economy, invention matters, but so do execution, scale, adaptation and the ability to learn faster than everyone else.
